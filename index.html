An earful of jaw: where the ossicles came from

Summary of Stephen Jay Gould's essay in Eight Little Piggies (1993), with notes on what to look at in this viewer.
The idea. New structures rarely appear from nothing. Evolution reuses parts that already do more than one job, and a part with a minor second role can take it over when its first role stops being needed. Gould's case study is the middle ear.
From gill arches to jaws. In early fishes each gill slit is supported by an upper and lower bar, hinged in the middle. The first such arch became the upper and lower jaws. Jaws and gill arches share their embryonic source (neural crest), their two-bar hinged shape and their muscles.
Stapes: second arch. The upper bar of the next arch became the hyomandibular, a strut bracing the jaw against the braincase. It sat right against the otic capsule, so it also carried vibration. In the earliest four-legged animals (Acanthostega, about 360 million years ago) the stapes was still stout and probably did bracing, breathing and some hearing at once. Once the braincase was fused to the skull, the brace was free to become a slender hearing bone.
Malleus and incus: first arch. In reptiles, amphibians and birds the jaw hinges between the quadrate (upper jaw) and the articular (lower jaw). In mammals these became the incus and malleus, and the jaw hinges instead between the squamosal and the dentary. Reichert showed this in 1837 in pig embryos: the back end of Meckel's cartilage ossifies and detaches to become the malleus. Marsupials still repeat the move after birth, in the pouch.
Never without a jaw. The objection that an in-between animal would have no working jaw fails because of redundancy. Advanced cynodonts had two jaw joints at once, the old quadrate–articular hinge and a new dentary–squamosal one (hence the name Diarthrognathus, "two-jointed jaw"). The old joint could then be handed over to hearing. Even the earliest mammals, such as Morganucodon, still had the malleus and incus attached to the jaw. Their ancestors' quadrate already touched the stapes, so it probably conducted sound long before it left the jaw.
In this viewer. The squamous part (red) is where the new mammalian jaw joint lives: the mandibular fossa that receives the condyle. Open the jaw with the Jaw slider and compare it with the malleus and incus a few millimetres medially, in the attic. Those two bones are the old reptilian jaw joint.
